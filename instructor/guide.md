# Instructor guide

This guide is for the person running the session. It says what to prepare, what to say, what to show, what mistakes to expect and how to turn each one into the lesson. Use it with three other pages:

- [`run-sheet.md`](run-sheet.md): the 90 planned minutes, minute by minute.
- [`answer-key.md`](answer-key.md): every answer, with its reason. Keep it open on your own screen, not the shared one.
- [`when-things-go-wrong.md`](when-things-go-wrong.md): what to do when the room does not go to plan.

**This kit has not yet been taught to a class.** The timings are planned, not measured. Treat your first run as a pilot: note where the minutes slip and where people get stuck, and adjust.

Words in *italics* under **Say** are a script you can read as written or put in your own words.

---

## What you need

- **6 to 16 people**, each with a laptop and a web browser. Nothing to install.
- **The participant folder** on each laptop, or on a shared drive they can open. Send it the day before if you can.
- **An assistant, or none.** Any chat assistant the participants' employer allows. If there is none, or it is blocked, every exercise has an answer ready in the workbook.
- **Know what the ready answers are.** They are real answers from one assistant (named, with the date, above each answer in the workbook) to the exact requests in `recorded/requests/`, kept exactly as they came back, notes and all. Two answers in exercise 3, B and C, are that assistant's answer A **edited** to plant the errors the exercise teaches; the workbook says so above each, and the answer key lists every change. If someone asks, say so plainly.
- **A screen** for your laptop.
- **The handout** (`participant/handout.html`), printed, one per person.

## Before the session

Allow about an hour, once.

1. **Do every exercise yourself** in the workbook, with the recorded answers. Then do exercises 1 and 2 again with the assistant the room will use, so you have seen what it does with this case. Assistants differ and change: a live answer may go wrong in other ways than the recorded one, and what you see is what the room will see.
2. **Read the answer key** once, in full. You do not need to remember it; you need to know where things are.
3. **Ask the host three questions.** Which assistant, if any, may people use for work? Is there a written policy on AI tools? Can the room's laptops open a local web page? (Some locked-down machines cannot. The single-file copy of the workbook usually works when the folder does not.)
4. **Try the workbook on one of the room's laptops**, with the network off. Open it, type in one box, press one *Check* button.
5. **Decide: live assistant or recorded answers.** If you are unsure the assistant will work in the room, plan for the recorded answers and treat a working assistant as a bonus. The session teaches the same thing either way, and the recorded answers are the ones this guide describes.
6. **Print the handout.**
7. **Set up your screen**: the workbook open at *Start here*; the answer key in a separate window that you do not share.

## The one idea, and how to use it

**The assistant drafts; you check; you decide.** Say it in the opening, come back to it after every exercise, and end on it. Each exercise is that sentence in a different situation:

| Exercise | The one idea, in this exercise |
|---|---|
| 1. From a wish to a brief | The draft is only as good as the request. |
| 2. Summarise, and prove it | Ask for a draft you can check, then check it. |
| 3. Find what is wrong | A draft that reads well can still be wrong. |
| 4. What must not go in | You decide what the assistant may see at all. |
| 5. Make it reusable | Keep the request that worked, so the next draft starts well. |

## How to run a debrief

Each exercise ends with a short debrief. It is where most of the learning happens, so protect its minutes.

1. Ask the **opening question** given for the exercise, to the whole room.
2. **Wait.** Count to five in your head before you say anything else. Someone will answer.
3. Take **two or three answers**. Ask "what did you see?" before "what should it be?".
4. **Add only what is missing.** Do not read the solution aloud; point to it.
5. **Name what you saw people do well**, specifically: "I saw several of you open message 4 before deciding." Specific praise teaches the room what good looks like.

Use the rubric ([`rubric.md`](rubric.md)) to know what to look for as you walk around. It is for your eyes; participants are not scored.

## Opening (5 minutes)

**Show:** the workbook, *Start here*.

**Say:**

*"In the next ninety minutes you will practise five things you can use at work tomorrow, with any AI assistant. We will use one small, invented case the whole time, so you will not have to learn something new for each exercise."*

*"There is one sentence I would like you to keep from today: the assistant drafts; you check; you decide. These tools are very good at producing a first draft quickly. They are not responsible for what you send. You are. Today is about doing that well, and quickly."*

*"Please open the workbook on your laptop. Everything you type stays on your laptop: nothing is sent anywhere. If you have an assistant you are allowed to use for work, open it in another tab. If you do not, that is fine: every exercise has an answer ready."*

*"Here is the case. You work in customer service at a small wholesale supplier of cups and packaging. A customer, the owner of a bakery, has had a late delivery that arrived short and partly damaged, and she has written to you several times. Open The case, then the e-mail thread, and skim it now. Two minutes. Do not try to remember the figures: it will be open all session."*

**Check:** ask for a hand from anyone who cannot see the thread. Solve that before you start exercise 1 (see *when-things-go-wrong.md*, "The workbook will not open").

## Exercise 1: From a wish to a brief

**Planned: 15 minutes. Objective O1: write a request as a five-part brief.**

**Purpose.** Participants see what a one-line wish really produces: not a wild answer, but a polished, plausible reply that quietly decides things for them. They find what it decided, what it claims happened that did not, and what it left open, then rewrite the request in five parts. They should leave knowing that the two parts people skip, the material and the limits, are the ones that matter most: where you set no limit, the assistant fills the gap itself.

**Show:** exercise 1, step 1: the reply.

**Say before (step 1):**

*"Most people start with a wish: write a reply to this customer. Let us see what a wish gets you. This is the reply one assistant wrote to exactly that one line, with the whole thread pasted under it. It is polished and polite. Read it as if you were about to press Send. Would you? Note everything it says or decides that nobody asked it to, anything it says happened that did not, and anything that stops you sending it as it is. Use the thread and the policy. Four minutes."*

**Then:** ask "Who would send it as it is?" Then "What did you find?" Take three or four answers. Expect people to find the three gaps in square brackets first, because they are easy to see. Press *Show the instructor's list*. Then press *Run the checker on this reply* and point at what it finds: one figure, 14, and it is in the assistant's own notes under the line of dashes, a date it worked out from "by Friday" in message 5. It is right by the calendar. Everything that is actually wrong passes the checker: the corrected invoice nobody decided, the receipt it says was sent, the same-day answer, the decision date promised on the manager's behalf. *"Those are decisions and claims, not figures. The checker is a helper. You are the judge."*

Then point at the notes under the reply: *"The assistant added its own notes. Did you read them? They catch the gaps, and the amount nobody has agreed. They say nothing about the invoice or the receipt. Read an assistant's caveats, and do not stop at them."*

**Say before (step 2):**

*"Now write the request again, in five parts. Write it to a capable new colleague who has never heard of this customer: they will do exactly what you write, and only that. The hint under each box says what goes there. When you are done, press Check. It tells you whether each part is there; it does not tell you whether it is good. Eight minutes."*

If the room has an assistant, add: *"If you finish, copy your brief, send it, and paste the new reply in step 3. Compare it with the first one."*

**While they work:** read the Limits boxes over people's shoulders. That is where the most useful help is.

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Stopping at the obvious problems** (the bracketed gaps, the asterisks) and missing the quiet decisions. *Turn it:* "The gaps are honest: the assistant tells you what it does not know. The risky lines are the ones that sound settled. Read every sentence that starts with 'We will' or 'I have', and ask: did anyone decide that? Did that happen?"
2. **Trusting the reply because it got the hard part right:** its figures are correct and it does not promise the credit. *Turn it:* "It got the credit right because the thread says plainly that the manager decides. Where the thread says nothing, about the invoice or the receipt, it filled the gap with a guess and wrote the guess as a fact. Your limits are how you stop that."
3. **Limits written as wishes:** "be careful with money", "be professional". The check finds no prohibition. *Turn it:* put "Be careful with money" next to "Do not promise anything the thread and the policy do not already commit us to: no credit, no change to the invoice, no delivery day", and ask which would have stopped the corrected invoice. Then ask them to rewrite one limit as a *Do not* sentence.
4. **The material box holds only her last message,** or **the form says "short and polite".** The check flags both. *Turn it:* "The assistant knows only what you paste." And: "Name a number, and say plain text if it goes into an e-mail: this reply came back with headings and asterisks."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"What did the one-line wish leave the assistant to decide, and which part of your brief now decides it instead?"*
- Follow-ups: "Which part was hardest to write?" (expect the limits). "What did the assistant's own notes catch, and what did they miss?" "Where did you get your limits from?" (the policy: the assistant cannot follow a rule it has not been given).

**Say after:**

*"A wish does not get you a bad reply. It gets you a plausible one that makes decisions you did not make: an invoice changed, a receipt sent, a same-day promise. A brief takes two minutes longer, and its limits are where you make those decisions yourself. The first card of your handout is the five parts: keep it by your keyboard for a week."*

**Solution:** `solutions/ex1-strong-brief.txt`, and a reply that follows it, `solutions/ex1-strong-reply.txt`. What the step 1 reply gets right and wrong, and what the checker finds in it: the answer key, exercise 1.

## Exercise 2: Summarise, and prove it

**Planned: 20 minutes. Objective O2: ask for an answer that can be checked, and check its figures and facts against the material.**

**Purpose.** Participants ask for a summary with a source for each fact, then check it against the thread. The recorded answer is a good one: it avoids the promise that was never given, keeps the two groups of cartons apart and cites every fact correctly. It still ignores the word limit, and one of its sentences says the opposite of the thread while passing every automatic check. That sentence is the most important moment of the session: **the checker points; a person reads.**

**Show:** exercise 2, step 1. Point at the last sentence of the Form part.

**Say before:**

*"Your manager has to decide today whether to give this customer a credit. She has two minutes. Here is a request that is ready to send. Look at the Form part: it asks the assistant to put the number of the message after every fact. That one line turns checking from a search into a glance."*

*"Send it, or use the recorded answer. Paste the answer and press Check. Then do the real work: for every Look again, open the thread and decide for yourself. Then read every sentence against the message it cites, including the ones the check passed. Last, write the version you would really send your manager, and check it again. Fifteen minutes."*

**While they work:** with the recorded answer, the check shows nine OK and one *Look again*, for the length. Walk the room and ask: "Once it is shorter, is it ready to send?" Let people say yes. Then ask them to read the sentence that begins "Tom's cause" against message 4.

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Treating the checker's verdict as the answer:** shortening the summary and sending it. *Turn it:* message 4 says the cartons "were left behind when the van was loaded". The summary says they "were left on the van at loading": the opposite. Every check passes it, because the figure is in the thread, the citation is right and nothing is merged. "The checker cannot read meaning. It points; you read."
2. **Shortening it by cutting the wrong lines,** often the line that no credit was promised, or the promise to confirm a delivery day. *Turn it:* "What does your manager have to decide? Keep what she needs for that: the amount, what we did and did not promise, what is still open. Cut the cause and the lids."
3. **Not asking why it ignored the limit.** *Turn it:* "The request said at most 120 words. It wrote far more. If an assistant ignores one limit you set, what else might it have missed? That is why you check good answers too."
4. **With a live assistant, the traps the recorded answer avoided:** the customer's claim reported as a fact ("Tom promised a credit"), or the two groups of cartons merged under one number. The check flags both. *Turn it:* "Who decides on the credit? If your summary says we already promised one, what is she likely to do?"

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"What would you change before you sent this to your manager, and how did you find it?"*
- Follow-ups: "Who found the sentence about the loading? How?" "What did the message numbers save you?" "If your manager had read the first version, what would she believe happened in our warehouse?"

**Say after:**

*"Two habits from this exercise. Ask for sources: one line in the request. Then read every sentence against its source, even the ones that pass every check. The checker found the length. Only reading found that one sentence says the opposite of the thread. The checker points; you read; you decide."*

**Solution:** `solutions/ex2-strong-summary.txt`. What a summary must hold, where each fact is, and what the recorded answer gets right and wrong: the answer key, exercise 2.

## Exercise 3: Find what is wrong

**Planned: 12 minutes. Objective O3: name what is wrong in an answer that looks right.**

**Purpose.** Participants mark every numbered sentence of two replies, A and B (eleven sentences in all): fine, a wrong figure, not in the material, or against the policy. **Answer A** is a real reply to the strong brief, exactly as it came back: it follows the brief and the policy, passes every automatic check, and still says two things that are not in the material. **Answer B** is the same reply, edited to plant the two kinds of error A lacks: a wrong figure and a breach of the policy. **Answer C** is for those who finish early, or homework. Participants should leave knowing three kinds of wrong, that a reply which obeys every rule can still invent, and that a machine finds some errors and not others.

**Show:** exercise 3, the list of four marks.

**Say before:**

*"Answer A is a real assistant's reply to the strong brief, the kind you wrote in exercise 1. Answer B is the same reply, with errors planted that A does not have. Mark every numbered sentence of A and B with one of the four marks: there are eleven. The assistant's notes under A are not numbered, but read them. Keep the thread and the policy open. Six minutes. Then press Check, and run the automatic check on A and on B. If you finish early, do answer C."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Marking all of A fine,** because it follows the brief and sounds careful. The sentences most likely to be missed are A6, which says the receipt was sent to the manager, and A7, a date the assistant chose itself. *Turn it:* run the automatic check on A: five OK. "A obeys every rule you gave it, and still says an action happened that nobody took. Its own notes under the reply admit it. Did you read them? Would a colleague who copies only the reply ever see them?"
2. **Arguing over A4 and A7.** The key accepts two marks for each. *Turn it:* "Both marks are defensible, and the reason says what tips it. The skill is to notice the question: 'next week' meant this week when message 4 was written, and the date in A7 came from the assistant, not the thread."
3. **Passing B3, because its figure is in the thread.** *Turn it,* in one sentence: "The figure check passes the 12 only because 12 is in the thread as the cup size, 12 oz; being in the material is not the same as coming from it, and the check of the two groups of six is what catches it."
4. **Marking B4 a wrong figure.** The amount is right; the promise is not allowed. *Turn it:* "Correct figures do not make a promise allowed. Which rule does it break?" (Rules 3 and 6.)

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"Which sentence would have cost the company most if the reply had been sent, and why?"* (Expect B4, a credit promised without approval. Some may say A6: the customer will believe the manager has her receipt when she does not.)
- Follow-ups: "Which errors did the automatic check miss, and why can it not find them?" "What did the assistant's own notes tell you?" "How would you change the brief to prevent each one?"

**Say after:**

*"Three kinds of wrong: a wrong figure, a fact from nowhere, and a promise you are not allowed to make. You catch the first by checking against the material. You catch the second only by reading, and a reply that follows every rule can still have it. You catch the third only if you know your own rules, and the assistant knows none of them unless you give them to it."*

**For those who finish early:** answer C is the same reply with a promised delivery day and a wrong figure that every automatic check passes: an amount from the thread, used for the wrong thing.

**Solution:** the answer key, exercise 3, which also lists every change made to B and C; and `solutions/ex3-annotated-answers.md`.

## Exercise 4: What must not go in

**Planned: 20 minutes. Objective O4: decide what must not be pasted into a public assistant, and prepare a table so that it can be.**

**Purpose.** Participants sort eight snippets into three piles, then prepare the orders table for an assistant by leaving out what the question does not need and replacing customers with stand-ins. They should leave able to sort before they paste, and knowing that leaving out is the strongest protection.

**Show:** exercise 4, the yellow line about the employer's policy.

**Say before (part 1):**

*"Before how to use an assistant, there is what you should never give it. Sort these eight snippets into three piles: fine to paste, paste after removing details, do not paste. For each one, ask two questions. Does the question need this detail? And who could be harmed if this text were read by someone else? Six minutes."*

*"One rule above all the others: your employer's policy comes first. If your company says no customer data in any assistant, that is the answer, whatever this key says."*

**Short debrief after part 1 (2 minutes):** *"Which snippet did you disagree with the key on?"* Expect the staff rota (it hides a health note) or the customer's complaint: the key accepts two piles for both, and the reason says what tips it.

**Say before (part 2):**

*"Now the useful part. Your manager wants to know which customers order most. That question needs the amounts and a way to tell customers apart. It does not need names, phone numbers or e-mail addresses. Use the helper: for each column, choose keep, replace with a stand-in, or leave out. Prepare the extract, check it, and if you have an assistant, ask it the question. Then paste its answer and put the real names back. The helper runs only in your browser. Ten minutes."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Keeping every column, or turning every column into a stand-in** (Phone 01, E-mail 01). *Turn it:* "What does the question need? Leaving a column out is stronger than any stand-in: what you do not paste cannot leak. Use stand-ins only where you need to tell rows apart."
2. **Putting the doctor's note in "remove details"**, by taking out the name. *Turn it:* "In a team of a dozen people, is it the name that tells you who this is?" Then show the alternative from the key: ask the question in general words.
3. **Over-correcting: putting everything in "do not paste"**, even the published price list. *Turn it:* "Refusing everything is not safe either. People then use the tools quietly, with no rules at all. The skill is to sort."
4. **Pasting the stand-in key along with the extract.** The check catches the names. *Turn it:* "Where is your key now? It stays on your computer. That is the whole point of a stand-in."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"What did you leave out of the table, and how did you decide?"*
- Follow-ups: "What would you do with a real customer list at work?" (check the policy first; take only what the question needs; stand-ins; or do not paste it at all). "Where is the key to your stand-ins now?"

**Say after:**

*"Sort before you paste: fine, remove details first, or never. The strongest move is leaving out what the question does not need. And this key is a teaching key, not legal advice: your employer's policy comes first. That is the third card of your handout."*

**Solution:** the answer key, exercise 4; `solutions/ex4-sorted-snippets.md`; and `solutions/ex4-extract.txt`, the extract the recommended choices produce.

## Exercise 5: Make it reusable

**Planned: 8 minutes, or as homework. Objective O5: turn a request that worked into a template a colleague can reuse.**

**Purpose.** Participants turn the summary request into a template with named placeholders and nothing left from this case. They should leave with one reusable request, and the habit of keeping requests that work.

**Say before:**

*"The summary request worked. Do not let it disappear into your chat history. Put it in the box, then replace everything that belongs to this case with a named placeholder in square brackets: the manager, the customer, the order number, and the whole thread becomes one placeholder. Keep what is true every time: the form and the limits. Add one line at the top that tells your colleague what to fill in and what to check afterwards. Five minutes, then press Check."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **A value of this case is left in**, often the manager's name or the order number inside the Task or the Form. The check lists each one. *Turn it:* "A value left in a template goes out with every use. Question 10 of the check is about exactly this."
2. **Blanks instead of names:** `___` or `[...]`. *Turn it:* "A name tells the next person what goes there. A blank makes them guess."
3. **Turning the limits into placeholders too.** *Turn it:* "The limits are the part you most want to keep. They hold your company's rules."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"Who in your team would use this template, and what would they need to know that is not in it yet?"*

**Say after:**

*"A request that works is a small asset. Name the placeholders, keep the rules, add a line on how to check the answer, and share it with your team."*

**If you are short of time,** set this exercise as homework: it needs no assistant and checks itself.

**Solution:** `solutions/ex5-strong-template.txt`.

## The ten-question check, and the close (10 minutes)

**Say:**

*"Ten questions, two for each skill we practised. Five minutes. Every option has an explanation, so read it even when you chose well."*

When most are done, ask: *"Which question made you stop and think?"* Take one or two answers. Do not ask for scores, and do not collect them.

**Close. Say:**

*"Three things to take away. First, a brief: situation, material, task, form, limits. Second, ask for answers you can check, and check them: every figure, every claim, every promise. Third, sort before you paste. The assistant drafts; you check; you decide."*

*"Your answers are on your laptop only. If you want to keep them, press Save my answers at the bottom of the workbook. And before you go: think of one task at work this week that you will try with a five-part brief."*

## After the session

- Participants keep the participant folder and the handout. Their answers are on their own laptops; the workbook sent nothing anywhere.
- If you want feedback, collect it separately. The workbook does not.
- Write down where the planned timings slipped and which exercises took longest. This plan has not yet been run with a class, and your notes are how it improves.
