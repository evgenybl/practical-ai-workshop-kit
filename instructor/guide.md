# Instructor guide

This guide is for the person running the session. It says what to prepare, what to say, what to show, what mistakes to expect and how to turn each one into the lesson. Use it with three other pages:

- [`run-sheet.md`](run-sheet.md): the 90 planned minutes, minute by minute.
- [`answer-key.md`](answer-key.md): every answer, with its reason. Keep it open on your own screen, not the shared one.
- [`when-things-go-wrong.md`](when-things-go-wrong.md): what to do when the room does not go to plan.

**This kit has not yet been taught to a class.** The timings are planned, not measured. Treat your first run as a pilot: note where the minutes slip and where people get stuck, and adjust.

Words in *italics* under **Say** are a script you can read as written or put in your own words.

---

## What you need

- **6 to 16 people**, each with a laptop and a web browser. Nothing to install.
- **The participant folder** on each laptop, or on a shared drive they can open. Send it the day before if you can.
- **An assistant, or none.** Any chat assistant the participants' employer allows. If there is none, or it is blocked, every exercise has an answer ready in the workbook.
- **Know what the ready answers are.** Until the recordings in `recorded/` are made, the answers in the workbook are **constructed stand-ins**: written for this kit to show the mistakes assistants commonly make with this case, not produced by an assistant. The workbook labels them so. If someone asks, say so plainly; it does not change what the exercise teaches.
- **A screen** for your laptop.
- **The handout** (`participant/handout.html`), printed, one per person.

## Before the session

Allow about an hour, once.

1. **Do every exercise yourself** in the workbook, with the stand-in answers. Then do exercises 1 and 2 again with the assistant the room will use, so you have seen what it does with this case. Assistants differ and change: what you see is what the room will see.
2. **Read the answer key** once, in full. You do not need to remember it; you need to know where things are.
3. **Ask the host three questions.** Which assistant, if any, may people use for work? Is there a written policy on AI tools? Can the room's laptops open a local web page? (Some locked-down machines cannot. The single-file copy of the workbook usually works when the folder does not.)
4. **Try the workbook on one of the room's laptops**, with the network off. Open it, type in one box, press one *Check* button.
5. **Decide: live assistant or stand-ins.** If you are unsure the assistant will work in the room, plan for the stand-ins and treat a working assistant as a bonus. The session teaches the same thing either way.
6. **Print the handout.**
7. **Set up your screen**: the workbook open at *Start here*; the answer key in a separate window that you do not share.

## The one idea, and how to use it

**The assistant drafts; you check; you decide.** Say it in the opening, come back to it after every exercise, and end on it. Each exercise is that sentence in a different situation:

| Exercise | The one idea, in this exercise |
|---|---|
| 1. From a wish to a brief | The draft is only as good as the request. |
| 2. Summarise, and prove it | Ask for a draft you can check, then check it. |
| 3. Find what is wrong | A draft that reads well can still be wrong. |
| 4. What must not go in | You decide what the assistant may see at all. |
| 5. Make it reusable | Keep the request that worked, so the next draft starts well. |

## How to run a debrief

Each exercise ends with a short debrief. It is where most of the learning happens, so protect its minutes.

1. Ask the **opening question** given for the exercise, to the whole room.
2. **Wait.** Count to five in your head before you say anything else. Someone will answer.
3. Take **two or three answers**. Ask "what did you see?" before "what should it be?".
4. **Add only what is missing.** Do not read the solution aloud; point to it.
5. **Name what you saw people do well**, specifically: "I saw several of you open message 4 before deciding." Specific praise teaches the room what good looks like.

Use the rubric ([`rubric.md`](rubric.md)) to know what to look for as you walk around. It is for your eyes; participants are not scored.

## Opening (5 minutes)

**Show:** the workbook, *Start here*.

**Say:**

*"In the next ninety minutes you will practise five things you can use at work tomorrow, with any AI assistant. We will use one small, invented case the whole time, so you will not have to learn something new for each exercise."*

*"There is one sentence I would like you to keep from today: the assistant drafts; you check; you decide. These tools are very good at producing a first draft quickly. They are not responsible for what you send. You are. Today is about doing that well, and quickly."*

*"Please open the workbook on your laptop. Everything you type stays on your laptop: nothing is sent anywhere. If you have an assistant you are allowed to use for work, open it in another tab. If you do not, that is fine: every exercise has an answer ready."*

*"Here is the case. You work in customer service at a small wholesale supplier of cups and packaging. A customer, the owner of a bakery, has had a late delivery that arrived short and partly damaged, and she has written to you several times. Open The case, then the e-mail thread, and skim it now. Two minutes. Do not try to remember the figures: it will be open all session."*

**Check:** ask for a hand from anyone who cannot see the thread. Solve that before you start exercise 1 (see *when-things-go-wrong.md*, "The workbook will not open").

## Exercise 1: From a wish to a brief

**Planned: 15 minutes. Objective O1: write a request as a five-part brief.**

**Purpose.** Participants see what a one-line wish produces, find what is wrong with it, and rewrite the request in five parts. They should leave knowing that the two parts people skip, the material and the limits, are the ones that matter most.

**Show:** exercise 1, step 1: the reply.

**Say before (step 1):**

*"Most people start with a wish: write a reply to this customer. Let us see what a wish gets you. Here is a reply to exactly that one line, with the whole thread pasted under it. Read it as if you were about to press Send. Would you? Note everything you would not send, and why. Use the thread and the policy. Four minutes."*

**Then:** ask "Who would send it as it is?" Usually few hands go up. Ask "What did you find?" and take three or four answers. Then press *Show the instructor's list* and *Run the checker on this reply*, and point out that the checker found the promises and the invented figure, but not that the reply ignores what she is most upset about. *"The checker is a helper. You are the judge."*

**Say before (step 2):**

*"Now write the request again, in five parts. Write it to a capable new colleague who has never heard of this customer: they will do exactly what you write, and only that. The hint under each box says what goes there. When you are done, press Check. It tells you whether each part is there; it does not tell you whether it is good. Eight minutes."*

If the room has an assistant, add: *"If you finish, copy your brief, send it, and paste the new reply in step 3. Compare it with the first one."*

**While they work:** read the Limits boxes over people's shoulders. That is where the most useful help is.

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **The material box holds only her last message, or nothing.** ("The assistant can see the thread.") The check says how many of the five messages it found. *Turn it:* "A new colleague handed only her last message would think we promised her a credit, because she says we did. The assistant knows only what you paste. Paste the whole thread."
2. **Limits written as wishes:** "be careful with money", "be professional". The check finds no prohibition. *Turn it:* put "Be careful with money" next to "Do not promise a credit or any amount" and ask which one would have stopped the reply in step 1. Then ask them to rewrite one limit as a *Do not* sentence.
3. **Form says "short and polite".** The check asks for a length or a structure. *Turn it:* ask three people how many words "short" is. You will get three different answers. "Name a number, and the assistant and you agree on what short means."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"Which of the five parts changed the reply most, and why?"*
- Follow-ups: "Which part was hardest to write?" (usually the limits). "What did your limits stop the assistant from doing?" "Where did you get your limits from?" (the policy: the assistant cannot follow a rule it has not been given).

**Say after:**

*"A brief takes two minutes longer than a wish, and saves you rewriting the answer. The parts people skip are the material and the limits, and they are the ones that matter most. The first card of your handout is the five parts: keep it by your keyboard for a week."*

**Solution:** `solutions/ex1-strong-brief.txt`, and a reply that follows it, `solutions/ex1-strong-reply.txt`. What is wrong with the step 1 reply: the answer key, exercise 1.

## Exercise 2: Summarise, and prove it

**Planned: 20 minutes. Objective O2: ask for an answer that can be checked, and check its figures and facts against the material.**

**Purpose.** Participants ask for a summary with a source for each fact, then check every figure against the thread. They should leave with two habits: ask for sources, and check what each figure means, not only whether it appears.

**Show:** exercise 2, step 1. Point at the last sentence of the Form part.

**Say before:**

*"Your manager has to decide today whether to give this customer a credit. She has two minutes. Here is a request that is ready to send. Look at the Form part: it asks the assistant to put the number of the message after every fact. That one line turns checking from a search into a glance."*

*"Send it, or use the stand-in answer. Paste the answer and press Check. Then do the real work: for every Look again, open the thread and decide for yourself whether to keep it, fix it, or take it out. Last, write the version you would really send your manager, and check it again. Fifteen minutes."*

**While they work:** look for people who stop at the checker's verdict. Ask them: "The checker says this figure is in the thread. Is it right?"

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Taking the checker's OK as "correct".** *Turn it:* "OK means every figure appears somewhere in the thread. It does not mean each one means what the summary says it means." Ask them to find a figure that appears twice in the thread with two different meanings. (The cartons that never arrived and the cartons that arrived crushed are the same number. See the answer key, exercise 2, "The two traps".)
2. **Reporting the customer's claim as a fact:** "Tom promised a credit." The stand-in answer does this, and the check flags it. *Turn it:* "Who decides whether we give a credit? Your manager. If your summary tells her we already promised one, what is she likely to do?" Then show the fix: "Mira says a credit was promised; Tom did not promise one (message 4)."
3. **Merging the two groups of cartons:** "the cartons were missing or damaged", with one number. *Turn it:* "How many cartons does she actually need replaced or credited?" The manager would underestimate the problem by half.
4. **Fixing a wrong citation by changing the figure, or a wrong figure by changing the citation.** *Turn it:* "Which one is wrong? Read the message to find out, then change only that."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"What did you change before you would send this to your manager, and how did you find it?"*
- Follow-ups: "What did the message numbers save you?" "What did the checker not catch that you did?" "If your manager had read the first version, what might she have decided?"

**Say after:**

*"Two habits from this exercise. Ask for sources: one line in the request. Then check every figure against them, and ask what each figure counts. A checker helps you find where to look. It cannot tell you whether a figure means what the summary says it means. That is you."*

**Solution:** `solutions/ex2-strong-summary.txt`. What a summary must hold, and where each fact is: the answer key, exercise 2.

## Exercise 3: Find what is wrong

**Planned: 12 minutes. Objective O3: name what is wrong in an answer that looks right.**

**Purpose.** Participants read three fluent replies and mark each sentence: fine, a wrong figure, not in the material, or against the policy. They should leave knowing three kinds of wrong, and that a machine can find some of them and not others.

**Show:** exercise 3, the list of four marks.

**Say before:**

*"These three replies answer a strong brief, the kind you wrote in exercise 1. They all read well. That is the problem. Mark every numbered sentence with one of the four marks. Keep the thread and the policy open beside them. Eight minutes. Then press Check. After that, press the buttons that run the automatic check on each answer, and see what it misses."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Marking almost everything Fine,** because the replies are polite and fluent. *Turn it:* "Reading for sense and reading for errors are different kinds of reading. For errors, read one sentence at a time, with the thread next to it."
2. **Missing the facts that are not in the material:** the driver's explanation and the loyalty discount in answer B. They are plausible, so they slip past. *Turn it:* run the automatic check on answer B: it finds nothing. "Answer B passes every automatic check and still has two invented facts. These are the most dangerous errors: a machine cannot find them, and they sound true."
3. **Calling sentence A2 fine because its number appears in the thread.** It does, as the number ordered, not the number delivered. *Turn it:* "Ask of every figure: what does it count?" The automatic check passes A2 for exactly the reason a careless reader does.
4. **Torn between two marks for C5,** a delivery day that is both promised against the policy and found nowhere in the thread. *Turn it:* "Both are accepted. Real errors overlap. What matters is that you would not send it."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"Which of these errors would have cost the company most if the reply had been sent, and why?"* (Usually a promise of money, A4 or C3, or the invented discount, B4, which the company must now honour or take back.)
- Follow-ups: "Which errors did the automatic check miss? Why can it not find them?" "How would you change the brief to prevent each one?"

**Say after:**

*"Three kinds of wrong: a wrong figure, a fact from nowhere, and a promise you are not allowed to make. You catch the first by checking against the material. You catch the second only by reading. You catch the third only if you know your own rules, and the assistant knows none of your rules unless you give them to it."*

**Solution:** the answer key, exercise 3, and `solutions/ex3-annotated-answers.md`.

## Exercise 4: What must not go in

**Planned: 20 minutes. Objective O4: decide what must not be pasted into a public assistant, and prepare a table so that it can be.**

**Purpose.** Participants sort eight snippets into three piles, then prepare the orders table for an assistant by leaving out what the question does not need and replacing customers with stand-ins. They should leave able to sort before they paste, and knowing that leaving out is the strongest protection.

**Show:** exercise 4, the yellow line about the employer's policy.

**Say before (part 1):**

*"Before how to use an assistant, there is what you should never give it. Sort these eight snippets into three piles: fine to paste, paste after removing details, do not paste. For each one, ask two questions. Does the question need this detail? And who could be harmed if this text were read by someone else? Six minutes."*

*"One rule above all the others: your employer's policy comes first. If your company says no customer data in any assistant, that is the answer, whatever this key says."*

**Short debrief after part 1 (2 minutes):** *"Which snippet did you disagree with the key on?"* Usually the staff rota (it hides a health note) or the customer's complaint.

**Say before (part 2):**

*"Now the useful part. Your manager wants to know which customers order most. That question needs the amounts and a way to tell customers apart. It does not need names, phone numbers or e-mail addresses. Use the helper: for each column, choose keep, replace with a stand-in, or leave out. Prepare the extract, check it, and if you have an assistant, ask it the question. Then paste its answer and put the real names back. The helper runs only in your browser. Ten minutes."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **Keeping every column, or turning every column into a stand-in** (Phone 01, E-mail 01). *Turn it:* "What does the question need? Leaving a column out is stronger than any stand-in: what you do not paste cannot leak. Use stand-ins only where you need to tell rows apart."
2. **Putting the doctor's note in "remove details"**, by taking out the name. *Turn it:* "In a team of a dozen people, is it the name that tells you who this is?" Then show the alternative from the key: ask the question in general words.
3. **Over-correcting: putting everything in "do not paste"**, even the published price list. *Turn it:* "Refusing everything is not safe either. People then use the tools quietly, with no rules at all. The skill is to sort."
4. **Pasting the stand-in key along with the extract.** The check catches the names. *Turn it:* "Where is your key now? It stays on your computer. That is the whole point of a stand-in."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"What did you leave out of the table, and how did you decide?"*
- Follow-ups: "What would you do with a real customer list at work?" (check the policy first; take only what the question needs; stand-ins; or do not paste it at all). "Where is the key to your stand-ins now?"

**Say after:**

*"Sort before you paste: fine, remove details first, or never. The strongest move is leaving out what the question does not need. And this key is a teaching key, not legal advice: your employer's policy comes first. That is the third card of your handout."*

**Solution:** the answer key, exercise 4; `solutions/ex4-sorted-snippets.md`; and `solutions/ex4-extract.txt`, the extract the recommended choices produce.

## Exercise 5: Make it reusable

**Planned: 8 minutes, or as homework. Objective O5: turn a request that worked into a template a colleague can reuse.**

**Purpose.** Participants turn the summary request into a template with named placeholders and nothing left from this case. They should leave with one reusable request, and the habit of keeping requests that work.

**Say before:**

*"The summary request worked. Do not let it disappear into your chat history. Put it in the box, then replace everything that belongs to this case with a named placeholder in square brackets: the manager, the customer, the order number, and the whole thread becomes one placeholder. Keep what is true every time: the form and the limits. Add one line at the top that tells your colleague what to fill in and what to check afterwards. Five minutes, then press Check."*

**Mistakes to expect, and how to turn each into the lesson**

1. **A value of this case is left in**, often the manager's name or the order number inside the Task or the Form. The check lists each one. *Turn it:* "A value left in a template goes out with every use. Question 10 of the check is about exactly this."
2. **Blanks instead of names:** `___` or `[...]`. *Turn it:* "A name tells the next person what goes there. A blank makes them guess."
3. **Turning the limits into placeholders too.** *Turn it:* "The limits are the part you most want to keep. They hold your company's rules."

**Debrief**

- **Opening question:** *"Who in your team would use this template, and what would they need to know that is not in it yet?"*

**Say after:**

*"A request that works is a small asset. Name the placeholders, keep the rules, add a line on how to check the answer, and share it with your team."*

**If you are short of time,** set this exercise as homework: it needs no assistant and checks itself.

**Solution:** `solutions/ex5-strong-template.txt`.

## The ten-question check, and the close (10 minutes)

**Say:**

*"Ten questions, two for each skill we practised. Five minutes. Every option has an explanation, so read it even when you chose well."*

When most are done, ask: *"Which question made you stop and think?"* Take one or two answers. Do not ask for scores, and do not collect them.

**Close. Say:**

*"Three things to take away. First, a brief: situation, material, task, form, limits. Second, ask for answers you can check, and check them: every figure, every claim, every promise. Third, sort before you paste. The assistant drafts; you check; you decide."*

*"Your answers are on your laptop only. If you want to keep them, press Save my answers at the bottom of the workbook. And before you go: think of one task at work this week that you will try with a five-part brief."*

## After the session

- Participants keep the participant folder and the handout. Their answers are on their own laptops; the workbook sent nothing anywhere.
- If you want feedback, collect it separately. The workbook does not.
- Write down where the planned timings slipped and which exercises took longest. This plan has not yet been run with a class, and your notes are how it improves.
