# When things go wrong

Practical fixes for a room that does not go to plan. Each entry says what you will see, what to do now, and, where it helps, how to turn it into part of the lesson. Read it once before the session; most fixes take a minute if you know them in advance.

---

### 1. There is no internet in the room

**You see:** assistants will not load; some people say the workbook "won't work".

**Do now:** the workbook needs no internet. Say so, and switch the whole room to the recorded answers: each exercise shows its answer directly or has a *Use the recorded answer* button. Run exercise 2 with the recorded answer for everyone, and in exercise 4, use *No assistant? Use an example answer* for the map-back.

**Lesson:** nothing is lost. The skills are briefing, checking and deciding, and the recorded answers are real answers with real mistakes worth finding.

### 2. An assistant refuses, or the employer blocks it

**You see:** "This site is blocked", a sign-in wall, or an assistant that declines the request.

**Do now:** do not try to get around a block: it is the employer's decision, and working around it in a training session sends the wrong message. Move those people to the recorded answers. If an assistant refuses a request, ask the person to read the refusal aloud; then use the recorded answer.

**Lesson:** a block is the employer's policy at work, which is exercise 4's first rule. Say: "That is exactly what we mean by 'your employer's policy comes first'."

### 3. Answers come back in another language

**You see:** a summary or reply in a language other than the request's, often because the assistant follows the browser's language or an account setting.

**Do now:** add one line to the Form part: "Write in English." Send again. If that does not work, use the stand-in.

**Lesson:** this is the Form part doing its job. Anything you do not state, the assistant decides for you, including the language.

### 4. An answer is far too long

**You see:** a "summary" of 400 words, or a reply of five paragraphs.

**Do now:** check that the person sent the prepared request unchanged. If they did, send it again with the length moved to the first line of the Form part. In exercise 2, let the checker's length rule show it.

**Lesson:** ask "Who decided how long this would be?" If you did not decide, the assistant did.

### 5. Someone pastes real company data

**You see:** a real customer name, a real contract, real figures in a box or, worse, in an assistant.

**Do now:** stop it quietly and at once. Ask them to delete it from the workbook box, and, if it went into an assistant, to delete that conversation and tell whoever handles such things at their employer, as their policy requires. Do not shame them in front of the room.

**Lesson (to the whole room, without naming anyone):** "A reminder for all of us: this workbook is for the invented case only. The question to ask before any paste is on the third card of your handout."

### 6. Someone finishes in a third of the time

**You see:** a participant waiting, or chatting, while others are halfway.

**Do now:** give them a stretch task for the exercise:
- **Exercise 1:** send the brief, then try to break it: change one limit and see what the assistant does.
- **Exercise 2:** write the summary for a different reader: the customer instead of the manager. What must change?
- **Exercise 3:** mark answer C, then rewrite the brief so that each of its errors becomes impossible.
- **Exercise 4:** write the three-line rule for their own team: what may go in, what must be cleaned, what never.
- **Exercise 5:** make a second template for a task they do every week.

Or pair them with someone who is stuck, if both agree.

### 7. Someone is lost

**You see:** a blank screen, a blank box, or a person who has stopped.

**Do now:** go to them, crouch to their level, and ask "Where are you?" rather than "Are you lost?". Point to the step number. If they are far behind, give them the recorded answer and tell them to skip to the step the room is on: the debrief will catch them up.

**Lesson:** for the person, not the room: "You don't need to finish every step. The step we discuss next is the one that matters."

### 8. The projector or screen fails

**You see:** nothing on the wall.

**Do now:** carry on. Participants have everything on their own laptops. Say the section name instead of showing it: "Everyone, go to exercise 2, step 1, and look at the Form part." Read the opening questions aloud. The guide's scripts work without a screen.

### 9. The room has mixed levels

**You see:** some people who have used assistants daily for a year, and some who have never opened one.

**Do now:** in the opening, say that the session is about checking and deciding, which is new for everyone, however much they have used the tools. Seat or pair experienced users with beginners for exercises 3 and 4. Use the stretch tasks (entry 6) for the fast ones.

**Lesson:** experienced users often make the confident mistakes, such as trusting a fluent answer. Ask them to explain in the debrief why answer A of exercise 3 passes every automatic check and is still wrong.

### 10. A participant insists the assistant is always right

**You see:** "Mine got it right, so I don't need to check", or "These tools don't make mistakes any more".

**Do now:** do not argue in general. Make it specific. Ask them to find, in the thread, the source of every figure in their assistant's answer, or to explain why the sentence about the loading in exercise 2 passes every check and is still wrong. Then ask: "Who signs the reply?"

**Lesson:** "Maybe your assistant was right today. Checking is how you know it was right, and you only find out it wasn't when you check."

### 11. The workbook will not open, or opens as text

**You see:** a page full of code, a "file blocked" message, or nothing at all.

**Do now:**
- If it opens as text in an editor: right-click `index.html`, choose *Open with*, and pick the browser.
- If the browser blocks the folder: open `participant/workbook-single-file.html` instead. It is the same workbook in one file.
- If the laptop blocks all local pages: pair the person with a neighbour, or print the case (`participant/case/`) and the handout.

### 12. Answers disappear

**You see:** "I came back and everything was gone."

**Do now:** the workbook keeps answers in the browser's own storage. A private window, a different browser, or a cleared browser loses them. Ask them to use a normal window. At the end, remind everyone of *Save my answers as a text file*.

### 13. The checker flags something the participant is sure is right, or misses something wrong

**You see:** "But that figure is right!" or "The checker said OK, but this is wrong."

**Do now:** agree with them if they are right. The checker looks for simple signs and says so on the page. A figure worked out from others (a sum) is not in the thread, so the checker asks you to check it. A figure that appears in the thread with a different meaning passes.

**Lesson:** this is the one idea in miniature. "The checker drafts a verdict; you check it; you decide."

### 14. Different assistants give very different answers

**You see:** two neighbours with quite different summaries from the same request.

**Do now:** use it. Ask both to run their answer through the check and compare. Ask the room: "If two answers to the same request differ, how do you know which one to trust?" (Answer: neither, until you check them against the material.)

### 15. You are running late

**Do now:** in this order: make exercise 5 homework; give everyone the recorded answer in exercise 2; shorten the part 1 debrief in exercise 4. Never cut the debriefs of exercises 2 and 3: they carry the lesson. See the run sheet, "If you are running late".

### 16. Someone challenges the privacy key

**You see:** "At my company we paste customer data all the time," or "That's too strict."

**Do now:** agree that the key is a teaching key, not legal advice, and that their employer's policy comes first, in both directions. If their employer has a contract with an assistant's provider that allows customer data, that is their policy. Then ask: "Does the question need the detail?" That test applies whatever the policy.

### 17. An assistant brings in facts from outside the material

**You see:** an answer that mentions the weather on the delivery day, an industry average, or a "typical" credit policy, often because the assistant searched the web or used a file from an earlier conversation.

**Do now:** treat it as "not in the material". Ask the person to add a limit: "Use only the e-mails below."

**Lesson:** a fact from outside the material may be true, but it is not checkable against your material, and the reader cannot tell it from what the customer actually said.
