# Model answers

One strong answer for each exercise, and why it is strong. "Strong" does not mean the only right answer: a participant's answer can differ and still be strong if each part does its job.

Every file here is run through the command-line checker in CI and must pass every rule (`expected.json` says so for each file). To try one yourself:

```
node tools/check.js 2 solutions/ex2-strong-summary.txt
```

## Why each is strong

**The brief (exercise 1).** Its *situation* says who is writing, who the customer is, and that she is partly right, which sets the tone. Its *material* is the whole thread and the policy, because the limits rely on the policy. Its *task* is one job. Its *form* names a length, a structure and the content of each paragraph. Its *limits* are four plain "Do not" sentences, each one taken from a rule of the policy, plus "Use only facts from the e-mails and the policy". Of the problems in the reply to the one-line wish, two are what these lines rule out: the corrected invoice, money off that the policy does not allow (the fourth limit; rule 2 sends missing goods on the next run), and the receipt it says was sent, a fact from nowhere (the last sentence). The second and third limits, no delivery day and no payment for the cups bought elsewhere, guard against mistakes that reply did not make. No line of the brief rules out its answer "the same day", or the decision date it promises on the manager's behalf.

**The reply (exercise 1, step 3).** It owns the missed promise first, says what is and is not known, promises only what the writer can do himself (to pass the request on today, and to write again by a day he names, even with no news), and promises no credit, no delivery day and no payment of other costs.

**The summary (exercise 2).** Every line that states a fact ends with the messages it comes from, and every citation is right; the last line names the decision, not a fact. It keeps the two groups of cartons apart. It says plainly that no credit was promised, and reports the customer's claim as her claim. It puts the decision the manager has to make last, in one line, and stays inside the word limit.

**The marks (exercise 3).** Each mark has a reason that points to a message or a rule of the policy. Where two marks are defensible, the key accepts both and the reason says what tips it: a delivery day both promised against the policy and found nowhere in the thread; a date the assistant chose itself, which is fine only once the writer has decided he can keep it; and "next week", which meant this week when the thread said it.

**The request (exercise 2, step 1).** One line in the Form part asks for the number of the message after each fact. It costs the assistant nothing, and it turns checking from a search into a glance.

**The piles and the extract (exercise 4).** Three snippets, the complaint, the orders table and the rota, have two defensible piles, and their reasons say what would tip each one to "do not paste". The extract keeps the four columns the question needs, replaces each business with a stand-in, and leaves out the contact person, the phone and the e-mail. Leaving out is stronger than any stand-in.

**The template (exercise 5).** Named placeholders for the material, the manager, the decision, the facts needed and the customer; nothing left from this case; the form and the limits kept, because they hold the company's rules. A first line tells the colleague what to fill in and what to check afterwards.
